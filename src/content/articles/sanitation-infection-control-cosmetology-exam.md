*By the PassCosmo team. Published July 15, 2026.*

Infection control is usually the largest single category on a state board cosmetology written exam, not a minor side topic. Texas's current PSI-administered exam weights Infection Control at 28% of scored questions, more than Licensing and Regulation (20%), Nail Care (6%), or Skin Care (6%) individually, and second only to Hair and Scalp Care overall ([PSI Texas candidate bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/703)). Minnesota's version of the exam uses a combined "Safety and Infection Control" category at 25%. If you're weak on sanitation content, you're weak on a quarter to nearly a third of the exam.

The content isn't abstract theory. It maps directly to the disinfection routine you're required to perform between every client, and it's graded separately from technique on the practical exam, meaning a technically good haircut can still lose points if the setup or cleanup steps are skipped.

## What does infection control actually cover on the exam?

Based on Texas's official candidate bulletin, the category covers cleaning and disinfecting implements, equipment, and the work environment; recognizing and responding to exposure incidents involving blood; conducting patch and strand tests before chemical services; and identifying skin, nail, hair, and scalp disorders that affect whether a service can proceed ([PSI Texas candidate bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/703)). Expect questions on the difference between cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting, on which implements require which level, and on how to handle a blood exposure incident during a service, since that's tested as its own practical exam section in most states, not folded into general cleanup.

## What's the actual legal basis for these rules?

State cosmetology boards write their own sanitation rules, but they sit on top of a federal baseline: OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030. Texas's own health and safety rules for cosmetology establishments require that products be "properly labeled in compliance with OSHA requirements," tying state salon rules directly to the federal standard rather than treating them as separate ([TDLR Barbering and Cosmetology Health and Safety Rules, July 2023](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/barbering-and-cosmetology/pdf/BAC-Health-and-Safety-Rules.pdf)). That's the practical reason blood exposure procedure shows up as a required demonstration on practical exams: it's not a cosmetology-specific invention, it's a workplace-safety requirement the licensing rule incorporates.

## What are the actual disinfectant rules?

Texas requires implements to be cleaned with soap and water first, then disinfected using either an EPA-registered disinfectant used exactly per its label instructions, or a chlorine bleach solution at specific concentrations and contact times ([TDLR Health and Safety Rules](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/barbering-and-cosmetology/pdf/BAC-Health-and-Safety-Rules.pdf)):

| Use case | Bleach concentration | Contact time |
|---|---|---|
| Low-level disinfection | 100 to 200 ppm | 10 minutes |
| High-level disinfection | 1,000 ppm | 20 minutes |
| Blood cleanup | 5,000 ppm | 5 minutes |

Disinfecting solution has to be mixed fresh daily, not reused from the day before, because disinfectant efficacy drops with age and repeated use. If you're using an EPA-registered commercial disinfectant instead of a bleach solution, follow the label's stated dilution and contact time exactly, since "close enough" concentrations are a common way products get flagged as ineffective on inspection.

## What are the most common sanitation violations state boards find?

TDLR's own published list of the ten most common code violations includes two sanitation-specific items directly: failing to prepare fresh disinfectant daily, and failing to store clean implements separate from soiled ones ([TDLR most common violations](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/barbering-and-cosmetology/establishments/most-common-violations.htm)). Both are easy to fix and easy to forget under a busy schedule, which is exactly why they show up repeatedly on inspection reports. If you're studying for the exam, these two are also a good gut-check for real-world habits, since they're the same failure points inspectors document most often, not just exam trivia.

## How do I actually study this category instead of just memorizing it?

1. Learn the three-tier hierarchy first: cleaning removes visible debris, sanitizing reduces germs to a safe level, disinfecting kills a broader range of pathogens on non-porous surfaces. Exam questions frequently test whether you can tell these apart, not just define them.
2. Memorize which implements require which level. Metal implements typically need disinfection; porous items like nail files are often single-use or client-specific rather than disinfected and reused.
3. Walk through the blood exposure procedure step by step until you can recite it without notes, since it's tested as a standalone practical exam section in most states.
4. Know your specific state's bleach concentrations and contact times cold if your state uses them as a benchmark, since the exact numbers (parts per million, minutes) are a common multiple-choice trap.
5. Review your state's published common-violation list if it publishes one. It tells you exactly where real practitioners lose points, which overlaps heavily with where exam-takers lose points too.

## FAQ

**What percentage of the cosmetology written exam is infection control?**
It varies by state, but it's typically one of the two or three largest categories. Texas weights it at 28% of scored questions; Minnesota's combined safety and infection control category sits at 25%.

**What's the difference between sanitizing and disinfecting?**
Sanitizing reduces the number of germs on a surface to a level considered safe. Disinfecting kills a broader range of pathogens, including bacteria and viruses, on non-porous surfaces. Cosmetology tools generally require disinfection, not just sanitizing, between clients.

**Do I need to know exact bleach concentrations for the exam?**
If your state uses bleach-based disinfection benchmarks (Texas does: 100 to 200 ppm for low-level, 1,000 ppm for high-level, 5,000 ppm for blood cleanup), expect specific concentration and contact-time questions. Check your own state's published rule for its exact numbers.

**Is the blood exposure procedure actually tested, or just a formality?**
It's tested directly. Most state practical exams include a required blood exposure incident demonstration as its own scored section, separate from the service you're performing when it comes up.

**What are the most common real-world sanitation violations at salons?**
According to TDLR's published violation data, the two most common sanitation-specific issues are not preparing fresh disinfectant solution daily and not storing clean implements separately from soiled ones.
