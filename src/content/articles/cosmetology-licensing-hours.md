Cosmetology licensing hours range from 1,000 to 1,800 in states that use a fixed school-hour requirement, while Oregon uses a competency-based path, according to [NIC's November 2025 analysis](https://nictesting.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/NIC-Analysis-2025-1-1.pdf). This 2026 reference lists all 50 states, separates school hours from alternate routes, and identifies the [12 states that had enacted the compact by June 2026](https://cosmetologycompact.gov/news/). Exam and license fees are shown only when a regulator publishes a clearly labeled current amount.

## How many cosmetology licensing hours does each state require?

Most states require 1,500 school hours for a full cosmetologist license. The National Interstate Council of State Boards of Cosmetology, or NIC, reported in its [November 2025 state licensing analysis](https://nictesting.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/NIC-Analysis-2025-1-1.pdf) that fixed requirements ranged from 1,000 hours in states such as California and Massachusetts to 1,800 hours in states such as Hawaii, Nebraska, and West Virginia.

The table uses the full cosmetologist or cosmetology-operator credential, not narrower hair-only, esthetics, nail, or barber licenses. "Alternate route" means the state reports an apprenticeship, experience, credit, or competency option; it does not mean an applicant can skip licensing.

## What do cosmetology exams and initial licenses cost?

There is no valid single 50-state "exam fee" comparison because states package charges differently. [California combines](https://www.barbercosmo.ca.gov/laws_regs/art12.shtml) an application and examination charge, [New York bills](https://dos.ny.gov/node/64686) its written and practical exams separately, and [Texas charges](https://tdlr.texas.gov/barbering-and-cosmetology/pdf/License-by-Examination-Application-BAC-LIC-002-E.pdf) a state application fee while its testing vendor handles exam scheduling.

## Does reciprocity let a cosmetologist work in another state?

Usually an out-of-state license can support an endorsement or reciprocity application, but it does not automatically authorize work in the destination state. Boards may compare school hours, exam history, active-license status, and work experience.

Kansas shows how conditional the word "reciprocity" can be. Its official guide says a cosmetologist with the full 1,500 hours and an active out-of-state license takes a 20-question open-book exam. An applicant with fewer hours may have to take board exams, complete missing education, or both, depending on license status. Kentucky asks for a license in good standing and either passing exam evidence or two years of continuous practice under its [reciprocal licensing regulation](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/titles/201/012/030/REG/).

The Cosmetology Licensure Compact adds a future multistate route. By June 2026, 12 states had enacted it: Alabama, Arizona, Colorado, Delaware, Kansas, Kentucky, Maryland, Ohio, Tennessee, Virginia, Washington, and West Virginia. The [Compact's official news page](https://cosmetologycompact.gov/news/) identifies Delaware as the 12th enacting state, while the [West Virginia announcement](https://cosmetologycompact.gov/2026/04/02/west-virginia-becomes-10th-state-to-enact-cosmetology-licensure-compact/) warns that licensees still could not practice through the compact while the commission built its operating system.

Enactment therefore does not equal an active multistate license. Until the commission announces that applications are open, use the destination board's existing endorsement process.

## How should applicants use this 50-state table?

Use the hours column to compare entry routes, then verify the destination board's application packet before paying a school or test vendor. Confirm the exact license name, whether the practical exam still applies, which education can transfer, and which fee recipient gets each payment.

The data also show why reciprocity decisions cannot be made by comparing hours alone. [California](https://www.barbercosmo.ca.gov/applicants/license_requirements.shtml) and [New York](https://dos.ny.gov/node/64686) both use 1,000-hour school requirements, yet their exam and endorsement procedures differ. [NIC reports](https://nictesting.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/NIC-Analysis-2025-1-1.pdf) that Oregon cannot be placed on the same clock-hour scale. The licensing board, rather than the school or a generic test-prep page, makes the final eligibility decision.
