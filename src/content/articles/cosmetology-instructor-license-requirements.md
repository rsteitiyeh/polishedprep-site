Cosmetology instructor license requirements build on top of an existing cosmetology license, not instead of one, and not every state actually requires a separate instructor credential to teach. Texas eliminated its standalone barber and cosmetology instructor licenses effective September 1, 2021; a Cosmetology Operator, Class A Barber, or Specialty license now covers teaching authority directly, no separate application needed ([TDLR](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/barbering-and-cosmetology/individuals/continuing-education.htm)). Georgia, by contrast, requires a fully separate instructor license with its own training-hour tiers on top of your Master Cosmetologist credential. Confirm which model your state uses before assuming either applies to you.

## Do you need an active cosmetology license first, everywhere?

Yes, in every state that licenses instructors separately, this is the non-negotiable starting point. You must hold a valid cosmetology (or relevant specialty) license in good standing before pursuing instructor training. There's no path to instructor licensure that skips standard licensure first.

## Why did Texas eliminate its separate instructor license?

TDLR folded instructor teaching authority into the existing Cosmetology Operator, Class A Barber, and Specialty licenses effective September 1, 2021, removing the standalone instructor credential entirely. In practice, this means a licensed Texas cosmetologist who wants to teach at a licensed school doesn't file a separate instructor application or complete a distinct instructor training program the way Georgia requires; the underlying operator license already authorizes instruction within the license holder's scope. This is a meaningful, fairly recent regulatory change, so don't rely on older guides that still describe a separate TDLR instructor license process.

## What does Georgia's instructor path actually require?

Georgia keeps a fully separate instructor license, governed by its own rule chapter, and the requirements are specific and tiered by specialty ([Georgia Rules and Regulations, Chapter 240-10](https://rules.sos.ga.gov/gac/240-10)):

| Instructor type | Required training hours | Prerequisite |
|---|---|---|
| Master Cosmetology Instructor | 750 hours, board-approved school | 1 year licensed as Master Cosmetologist + 1 year documented salon work experience |
| Esthetician Instructor | 500 hours, board-approved school, minimum 9 months | 1 year licensed + 1 year documented work experience |
| Nail Technician Instructor | 250 hours, board-approved school, within 4 months | 1 year licensed + 1 year documented work experience |

On top of the hour requirement, Georgia requires a high school diploma or GED, proof of the prerequisite license and experience, and passing a written and practical instructor exam in English before the board issues the license.

## Why does the hour requirement scale down by specialty in Georgia?

Because Georgia sets instructor training hours in rough proportion to the underlying license's own scope: Master Cosmetology, the broadest license, requires the most instructor hours (750), while Nail Technician, the narrowest, requires the fewest (250), with Esthetician (500) in between. The pattern mirrors the same logic that separates initial licensing hours across those three credentials, a broader service scope means more content an instructor has to be prepared to teach.

## What's the actual timeline to get there?

1. **Hold an active cosmetology (or relevant specialty) license**, in states that require a separate instructor credential.
2. **Build the required work experience**, one year documented salon or shop experience in Georgia, though other states set different thresholds.
3. **Complete a board-approved instructor training program**, 250 to 750 hours in Georgia depending on specialty; confirm your own state's hour count directly, since this varies meaningfully.
4. **Pass the instructor licensing exam**, written and practical, in states that require one.
5. **Apply for the instructor license**, or, in a state like Texas, confirm your existing operator-level license already covers instruction and skip this step entirely.

## Does every state require a separate instructor exam?

In states that maintain a distinct instructor license, generally yes, a written and practical exam focused on teaching methodology and classroom or clinic-floor management, separate from the technical exam you passed for your original cosmetology license. Georgia requires this explicitly as part of its instructor application. Texas, having eliminated the standalone credential, doesn't require a separate instructor exam at all, since instruction authority now comes bundled with the underlying operator license.

## How does this interact with renewal?

Instructor licenses in states that issue them separately generally renew on their own schedule, sometimes different from the underlying cosmetology license's cycle. Georgia's cosmetology instructor licenses expire June 30 of odd-numbered years, a different fixed date than the August 31 deadline that applies to its nail technician licenses. If you're pursuing instructor licensure, check whether your state uses a shared or separate renewal calendar so you're not caught tracking the wrong date.

## FAQ

**Do I need a separate license to teach cosmetology?**
It depends on the state. Georgia requires a fully separate instructor license with its own hour and exam requirements. Texas eliminated its standalone instructor license effective September 1, 2021; an existing Cosmetology Operator, Class A Barber, or Specialty license now covers teaching authority.

**How many hours of instructor training does Georgia require?**
It depends on specialty: 750 hours for Master Cosmetology Instructor, 500 for Esthetician Instructor, 250 for Nail Technician Instructor, each on a board-approved school's schedule.

**How much work experience do I need before pursuing instructor training in Georgia?**
One year licensed as a Master Cosmetologist (or the relevant specialty license) plus one year of documented work experience in a salon or shop setting.

**Is the instructor exam the same as the standard cosmetology exam?**
No, in states that require one. Georgia's instructor exam tests teaching methodology and classroom management in addition to relevant technical content, distinct from the exam passed for original licensure.

**Why did Texas drop its separate instructor license?**
TDLR consolidated instructor teaching authority into the existing Cosmetology Operator, Class A Barber, and Specialty licenses effective September 1, 2021, removing the standalone credential and its separate application process.
